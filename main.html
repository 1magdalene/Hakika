A card that speaks when memory fails

A simple, secure way to help strangers know who to call when someone who wanders — an elder living with dementia, a child on the autism spectrum, or anyone else who struggles to communicate or find their way — is found away from home.

See how it works Who sees what

Hakika is a caregiver-managed profile system built around a simple problem: when someone wanders — an elder living with dementia, a child on the autism spectrum, or anyone else who struggles to communicate or find their way home — and is found by a stranger, there's usually no way for that stranger to know who to call or how to help.

Hakika lets a caregiver create a profile for the person in their care — condition notes, safe instructions, and emergency contacts — which gets printed directly onto a card, bracelet, or lanyard tag, along with a QR code. Anyone who finds them can read the essentials straight off the card, no phone required — name, a brief condition summary, one safe instruction, and the caregiver's contact. The QR code is a bonus: scanning it opens the same profile online, kept current even if something's changed since the card was printed.

Real-Life example

Being found isn't the hard part. Being understood is.

An elder living with dementia steps out for what she thinks is a short walk. Two hours later she's at a matatu stage three estates from home, and she can't say her name today, let alone her son's phone number.

Nobody who finds her is unwilling to help. They just have nothing to act on — no contact, no context, no way to tell "confused" from "in danger."

Caregivers already hold this information in their heads. Hakika just puts it somewhere a stranger's phone camera can reach it.

Why this keeps happening

The gap isn't awareness of the problem, It's a system to act on it.

  • Diagnosis comes late Families often dismiss early memory loss and confusion as ordinary aging, and in some cases attribute it to spiritual causes — both delay the point at which the family gets a diagnosis, let alone a safety plan.
  • Care is informal and unsupported Dementia care in Kenya happens almost entirely inside the home, carried by family members or informally paid domestic workers, with little formal training or backup.
  • No shared system exists yet There's no national identification or wandering-response system for people with dementia in Kenya — caregivers are left to build their own ad hoc solutions, like writing a name and number on a piece of paper.

Grounded in Kenya-based research on dementia caregiving, including studies from the Aga Khan University Brain and Mind Institute and a qualitative study of caregivers across three Kenyan counties. For a firsthand account, see the Nation Africa article Behind closed doors: the struggles of caring for dementia patients.

Why register at all?

A handwritten card works — until it doesn't.

Writing a name and a phone number on a piece of cardboard costs nothing, and for a single card, printed once, it does almost the same job. An account earns its place in what happens next.

  • It stays current A handwritten card is frozen the moment it's written. Phone numbers change, conditions progress, instructions stop fitting — and the card just keeps confidently showing whatever was true on day one. With an account, updating means editing one field; the card's QR always opens whatever is current, without anyone needing to notice it's gone stale and reprint it.
  • It covers more than one person A caregiver looking after several people — a JAF volunteer, a care home worker — has no way to track several handwritten cards at once: which is old, who still needs one printed. One dashboard replaces an untracked stack of cardboard.
  • It builds in the privacy split A blank piece of card gives a rushed caregiver no structure, which is exactly how sensitive details end up written on it by mistake. Hakika's form enforces the public/private split by design, so getting that judgment call right doesn't depend on the caregiver remembering to.
  • It survives loss Cards get lost, rained on, chewed by a goat. With an account, the caregiver logs back in and reprints from what's already saved, instead of reconstructing everything from memory.
